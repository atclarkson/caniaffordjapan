Harper's 4th birthday in Osaka, June 2023, ended up at Round1, the same bowling-and-arcade chain we've written up Sakai pricing for separately. What made this particular visit worth a post on its own: the shoes didn't come from a counter or a staff member at all, they came out of a vending machine.

## How it works

The setup is exactly what it sounds like, and it's a real, documented feature of Round1 locations rather than a one-off at this specific branch: a row of machines along the shoe-rental wall, each one labeled with a shoe size in centimeters, a button on the front, and an opening at the bottom. Press the button for your size, the shoes drop out. No counter, no staff member measuring feet, no waiting in a line while someone digs through a shelf. Sizing tops out around 27.5 to 28 centimeters on the machines themselves, anyone needing something larger still goes to a staff member directly, so the automation covers most adults and every kid without replacing the option entirely.

## The chain's own roots explain the vending machine

Round1 didn't start as a bowling alley at all. The company's predecessor, Sugino Kosan, opened in Izumiotsu, Osaka Prefecture, in December 1980 as a roller rink paired with arcade games, bowling lanes weren't added until 1982, two years in. The company renamed itself Round1 in September 1993 and has since grown to over 150 locations in Japan, plus a real US expansion starting with a 2010 location outside Los Angeles. A chain built on coin-op arcade machines first and bowling second is exactly the kind of business that would reach for a vending machine to solve shoe rental rather than hiring someone to hand pairs across a counter, automation is the house style here, not a one-off gimmick at this specific branch.

## What it cost, cross-referenced rather than re-derived

We've already written up [current Round1 pricing in detail from our Sakai visits](/blog/round1-bowling-arcade-cost-japan-family/), the straight-bowling-versus-arcade-pass math that applies at any Round1 location in Japan, this one included. Nothing about the vending-machine shoe rental changes that math, it's the same per-person shoe fee either way, just handed over by a machine instead of a hand.

## Was it worth it

A kid's birthday spent half at the lanes and half feeding coins into claw machines is a good birthday by any measure, vending-machine shoes or not. What makes it worth writing up separately is the small, specific detail a standard pricing post would skip past: a chain built around self-serve automation down to the footwear, a real, small piece of how Japan does convenience differently, and exactly the kind of thing that turns a routine bowling trip into something a four-year-old still gets a whole vlog out of.
