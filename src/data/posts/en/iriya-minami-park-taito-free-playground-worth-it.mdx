May 12, 2024, a Taito park neither of us had heard of before that afternoon, and the girls disappeared up an artificial hill for the better part of an hour while we stood at the bottom wondering whether we'd stumbled onto the best free playground we'd find in Tokyo or just gotten lucky with good weather and tired kids. A year and a half of comparing it against everything else we've covered on this site since, the answer isn't close.

## What's built into the hill

Iriya Minami Park sits on about 3,910 square meters in Taito, renovated into its current layout in 2013, and the centerpiece is a real artificial hill rather than a flat lot with equipment scattered across it. A jungle gym connects into a bouldering wall, which leads into a mesh tunnel climb, which lets out at the top of two separate slides built side by side into the same hillside, one wide enough for two kids at once, the other taller and longer. None of it is ticketed or timed, and the park has no closed days at all, free and open every day of the year.

## The neighborhood this park sits in

Iriya, the ward this park belongs to, has its own real flower-growing history that has nothing to do with playgrounds. From the mid-1800s through the early Taisho era, roughly the 1910s, Iriya was a center of Tokyo's flower industry, with breeders competing to produce increasingly impressive morning glory varieties and exhibiting them at annual markets. Those markets got popular enough in the early 1890s that foot traffic reportedly ground to a halt in the neighborhood. The tradition lapsed for decades before a group of locals and the Shitaya Tourism Association revived it in 1948, and the Iriya Asagao Matsuri, the Morning Glory Festival, still runs every July 6 through 8 today, around 120,000 plants from 60-some vendor stalls drawing roughly 400,000 visitors to the same streets near Iriya Station over three days. None of that history is visible from inside the playground itself, but it's worth knowing the neighborhood has its own real reason to be on a visitor's radar beyond the slides, if the timing happens to line up with early July.

## How this compares to the other free playgrounds we've covered

We've already written up a small, unnamed Katsushika playground tucked under an elevated train line, the kind of find that works because it's a five minute walk from wherever you're staying, not because it's anything special on its own. That one is swings, a slide, open ground, the kind of generic setup that exists in some form in nearly every residential neighborhood in the country. Iriya Minami Park is a different category entirely, a named, purpose-built destination worth the trip on its own terms rather than a lucky find along the way, the kind of park a family would plan an afternoon around rather than stumble into on a walk to somewhere else.

It's closer in ambition to Ota's Heiwa no Mori Park, which we've also covered, the one with a genuine engineered climbing structure. The difference is in how each park handles age and cost. Heiwa no Mori splits its equipment into a free toddler area, sized for kids too young for elementary school, and a separate 360 yen course built for roughly ages 8 to 13, a real age gap families need to plan around between the two. Iriya Minami Park doesn't split anything by age or by price. The bouldering wall, the tunnel, both slides, all of it sits on the same free side, open to whichever kid in a family wants to try it, which is the real difference once you've compared all three parks side by side rather than just the headline fact that all three cost nothing to walk into.

## Getting there

Iriya Station on the Tokyo Metro Hibiya Line puts you about a 6 minute walk from the gate, close enough that it works as a stop on the way to or from Ueno or Asakusa rather than a special trip across the city. That's a shorter, simpler route than either of the other two playgrounds we've covered, neither of which sits this close to a subway line serving the main tourist circuit.

## What to bring

There's no shop or vending machine inside the park itself, so a water bottle and a snack are worth carrying in rather than counting on finding something nearby, especially on a warm afternoon when a climbing structure built into a hill holds heat differently than flat ground does. The slides are enclosed metal, which gets hot enough in direct sun to matter, worth checking with a hand before letting a kid in shorts go down it on a bright day. None of this is a real obstacle, just the kind of detail that's easy to miss walking in without having read about the park first, the way we did.

## Was it worth the stop

Yes, and it's the one we'd point a family toward first if they could only make time for one free playground in Tokyo. The Katsushika find works because it costs nothing and needs no planning, and Heiwa no Mori Park is worth a half day if a kid is old enough for the paid course, but Iriya Minami Park gives you a real engineered adventure structure, climbing, a tunnel, two slides, without a single yen changing hands or a single age restriction to check first. We didn't plan the stop and ended up staying far longer than we expected to, which is usually the best sign a free attraction was worth finding.
