We wandered into MOOOSH Squishy near Takeshita Street in Harajuku on a June 2024 afternoon without really planning to, the kind of find that happens when three kids spot a wall of soft, brightly colored characters through a shop window and refuse to keep walking. It turned into one of the more entertaining half hours of that trip, and one of the cheaper ones too, once we understood how the store actually works.

## The brand behind the store

iBLOOM has been making squishies since the early 2000s, when it mass-produced the first commercial batch of slow-rising squishy toys and helped push what had been a niche novelty into a genuine mainstream toy category in Japan. It's now the top brand in the amusement squishy market, known for a realistic bread-and-sweets line, alongside its own original characters, and a house style built around three things: an overwhelmingly soft, slow-rise texture, character designs with an actual visual identity rather than generic blobs, and a pleasant scent baked into the foam itself. None of that history costs anything to know walking in, but it explains why a shop selling foam toys draws a line with numbered tickets in the first place.

## Getting in the door isn't automatic

MOOOSH is the official specialty store for iBLOOM, the brand behind Japan's most popular squishy toy line, soft, scented, slow-rising foam characters that are meant to be squeezed and smelled as much as collected. The catch: it's popular enough that the store runs on a numbered ticket system rather than just letting people walk in whenever. Even showing up in the early afternoon, we saw a real line of people holding numbered tickets waiting for their turn, and same-day tickets can run out before the afternoon does. If you want in, morning is the safer bet.

## What's actually free

Once you're inside, browsing costs nothing. Part of the store's whole appeal is hands-on, you're encouraged to pick up, squeeze, and smell the squishies before deciding whether to buy, not just look at them behind glass. For three kids who'd never encountered a store built entirely around a toy they're allowed to handle first, that alone was worth the wait in line.

## What costs money

Prices scale with size and rarity rather than sitting at one flat number:

| Item | Typical price |
|---|---|
| Mini squishies | Around 400 yen |
| Gacha capsule | Around 400 yen |
| Larger, premium squishies | Higher, priced individually per character |

The gacha machines set up inside the store are a genuine bonus mechanic on top of regular retail shopping, drop in a coin and get a random mini squishy rather than picking one off the shelf. MOOOSH also runs an in-store lottery system during busier periods, structured so that everyone who plays wins something, with the grand prize being an extra-large version of a squishy rather than a miss-and-walk-away outcome. It's a real cost on top of browsing, but a low-stakes one, nothing here runs into the territory of a splurge purchase.

## Getting there

MOOOSH sits just off Takeshita Street, Harajuku's main pedestrian shopping strip, a short walk from Harajuku Station on the JR Yamanote Line. It's easy to fold into a day that already includes Takeshita Street's crepe stands and the walk toward Meiji Shrine, all within the same few blocks, and the numbered ticket system means it's worth deciding early in the day whether you actually want to commit the time, rather than treating it as a five-minute detour off the main strip.

## Was it worth it

For the cost of a coin-operated gacha pull and a few mini squishies split three ways, yes, easily. The real lesson wasn't the price, it was the queue: knowing ahead of time that a numbered ticket stands between you and the door would have saved us some standing around, and it's the one thing about this stop worth planning around rather than discovering on the sidewalk. Three kids walked out with something soft and scented to squeeze on the train home, and none of it cost enough to think twice about.
