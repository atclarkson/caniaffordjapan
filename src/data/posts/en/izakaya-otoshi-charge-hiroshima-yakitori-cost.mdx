Sit down at almost any izakaya in Japan and a small dish arrives before you've ordered anything, pickles, edamame, something simple, nobody asked for it and nobody explains it. That's otoshi, and it's not free. It shows up on the bill whether you touch it or not, usually 300 to 500 yen per person, occasionally closer to 1,000 yen at a nicer spot, and it's the one real surprise charge worth knowing about before your first Japanese izakaya dinner rather than after.

## What otoshi is

It functions as a table charge more than an actual appetizer, Japan has no tipping culture, and otoshi is one of the ways an izakaya recovers the cost of seating you and getting a drink in front of you quickly. It's rarely listed on the menu and rarely explained when you sit down, the dish just appears, and even leaving it completely untouched doesn't remove it from the bill. If the number matters to you, asking staff directly, "otoshi wa ikura desu ka," how much is the otoshi, before you sit down is a real, workable way to know ahead of time rather than finding out on the receipt. Some places will let you decline it if you ask before ordering, though a few substitute a flat seat charge instead, so declining doesn't always mean paying nothing.

## What a real Hiroshima yakitori dinner runs

We don't have an itemized receipt from our own evening, no specific restaurant name to point to, so here's the honestly-researched range rather than a guessed number: per-person dinner at a typical Hiroshima yakitori izakaya runs roughly 2,000 to 3,000 yen, moving up to 3,000 to 4,000 yen at a step-up spot, and 4,000 to 5,000 yen at the pricier end. On the cheap end, some chains advertise individual yakitori skewers from around 88 yen each with all-day beer specials near 218 yen, a real budget option if you're building a meal skewer by skewer rather than ordering a course. Add otoshi's 300 to 500 yen on top of whichever tier you land in, it's rarely included in these per-person estimates since it's charged separately rather than folded into a menu price.

## How Hiroshima compares to what we've found elsewhere

We've already written about izakaya pricing in Osaka, where individual yakitori skewers run 100 to 300 yen each at a typical spot, Torikizoku's nationwide chain prices its whole menu at a flat 390 yen a plate, and a standard draft beer lands between 500 and 800 yen. Hiroshima's per-person totals above sit in roughly the same territory once you add up a full meal rather than comparing single skewers, core food pricing for this category of casual dining stays fairly consistent nationwide. That earlier Osaka post didn't mention otoshi at all, not because it doesn't apply there, but because the charge is common enough everywhere in Japan that it's worth its own explanation rather than assuming either city's post already covered it.

## What else that evening cost

The same day included crane games and Christmas shopping before dinner. Crane and UFO catcher machines run close to 100 yen a play as a rough baseline, the same figure we've already found true of arcade crane machines elsewhere in Japan, coin-operated by default rather than card-friendly. No specific numbers exist for the Christmas shopping itself, that part of the evening doesn't have a receipt or a clear total attached to it in anything we filmed, so we're not going to invent one.

## Was it worth it

Yes, and the otoshi charge is exactly the kind of small, easily-overlooked cost that makes a bigger difference to how a first Japanese izakaya dinner feels than its actual yen value. Walking in already knowing a small, unordered dish is coming, and that it's a real charge rather than a mistake on the bill, turns a potential moment of confusion into a normal part of the meal. A Hiroshima yakitori dinner in the 2,000 to 5,000 yen range, otoshi included, is a real, worthwhile way to close out a long day, which is exactly what ours was.
