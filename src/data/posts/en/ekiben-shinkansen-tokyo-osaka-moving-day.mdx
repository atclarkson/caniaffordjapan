May 23, 2023 wasn't a sightseeing day. It was a moving day, checking out of a Tokyo hotel, hauling bags to the station, and riding the Shinkansen to a new home base in Osaka, [not our first bullet train ride](/blog/first-shinkansen-ride-tokyo-osaka-mt-fuji-history/) but a different one, months later, with a full household's worth of luggage instead of a day trip's. Lunch that day wasn't a restaurant stop, it was whatever we could buy on the platform before boarding and eat at 285 km/h.

## What ekiben is

Eki means station, ben is short for bento, so ekiben is just station bento, boxed meals sold specifically for eating on a train rather than picked up at any ordinary convenience store. Tokyo Station alone stocks something like 170 different kinds at its main ekiben shop, everything from a plain rice-and-grilled-fish box to elaborate regional specialties built around whatever that area of Japan is known for. It's less a single dish than an entire category, different every time depending on which station you're buying from.

## What we got

Four different boxes for five people: fried chicken for Adam, dumplings for Lily and Lindsay to split, and Cora and Harper each got a themed box built around Dr. Yellow, the real inspection Shinkansen that checks the Tokaido and Sanyo lines' tracks and overhead wires, painted bright yellow instead of the usual white, and considered good luck to spot from a platform since sightings are rare. Tokyo Station has sold Dr. Yellow themed ekiben on and off for years, the exact box and collaboration character rotates, so don't expect the same one we had, just know the train-themed option is a real, recurring thing to look for if a kid in your group is into trains.

## What it costs

A typical Tokyo Station ekiben runs somewhere around 700 to 1,800 yen depending on how elaborate the box is, with most everyday options landing closer to the 1,000 to 1,200 yen mark. Call it $5 to $12 a box at typical exchange rates, for four boxes across five people that's a real lunch for well under what a sit-down meal would run, without losing a single minute of the ride to a dining car that the Tokaido Shinkansen doesn't even have.

## Why it's worth doing over a station convenience store run

A convenience store lunch works fine for a quick trip, we've leaned on one plenty of times ourselves. Ekiben is the better call specifically when the ride itself is part of the day, a two and a half hour stretch between Tokyo and Osaka is long enough that a real meal, train-themed box included, turns the travel time into part of the experience instead of just time to get through. [Buying the ticket right is its own real cost question](/blog/booking-shinkansen-tickets-japan-cheapest-way/), but the lunch on top of it is one of the cheaper, more memorable calls you'll make on a Japan moving day.
