Two days before leaving Japan at the end of a long trip, we ended up at a Kura Sushi table watching plates circle past on a belt, and one of the kids asked the obvious question none of us had a real answer for: how does this work? Turns out the answer is a real, specific invention story, not just "it's a conveyor belt," and it's worth knowing before your next visit to any kaiten-zushi chain, not just this one.

## A beer brewery, maybe

The most commonly repeated origin story credits Yoshiaki Shiraishi, a restaurant owner in Higashiosaka, with getting the idea while watching beer bottles move along a conveyor belt at an Asahi brewery, several sources flag this specific detail as closer to legend than documented fact, worth saying honestly rather than presenting as settled history. What's better documented: Shiraishi spent years, sources differ on whether it was four or closer to ten, developing the belt speed, the restaurant layout, and the whole format before opening the first conveyor belt sushi restaurant, Mawaru Genroku Sushi, in Higashiosaka in 1958. That's the real starting point for every kaiten-zushi restaurant operating in Japan today, Kura Sushi included.

## The pricing system is also his invention

Shiraishi didn't just solve how to move sushi past customers, he solved how to bill for it: color-coded plates, where the plate's color or pattern tells the register what price tier that dish belongs to, letting a customer eat as much or as little as they want and get billed by counting plates at the end rather than working from a written order. That system, invented alongside the belt itself, is still exactly how Kura Sushi and nearly every other kaiten-zushi chain in Japan prices a meal today, nothing about the color-coded plate has fundamentally changed since 1958.

## Why it took until the 1970s to spread

Shiraishi held a patent on the conveyor belt mechanism, which meant for roughly fifteen years after that first Higashiosaka restaurant, nobody else could legally open a competing version. Kaiten-zushi only took off nationwide once that patent expired in the mid-1970s, other restaurateurs free to copy and build on the format for the first time. There's a real irony in how that played out: Shiraishi's own company, the one that started it all, had grown to around 250 locations at its peak but had shrunk to just 11 by 2001, outcompeted by the very chains his own expired patent let into the market.

## What's new: the plates fight back against bacteria

The belt and the color-coded plates are 1958 technology, still running basically unchanged. What's real and new, and specific to Kura Sushi rather than the format generally, is a hygiene cover system the chain calls Sendo-kun ("Mr. Fresh"), domed plastic covers that snap onto each plate as it travels the belt. A customer doesn't lift the dome by hand, the cover pops open automatically the moment you grab the plate underneath it. Each cover has an embedded RFID chip that tracks how long that specific plate has been circulating and reports back to the kitchen, both a freshness-monitoring tool and the mechanism that flags a plate for removal once it's been on the belt too long. The covers meet the ISO 22196 international antibacterial material standard, developed specifically to protect the food from airborne bacteria, viruses, and dust while it travels past dozens of tables. The system dates back to 2011 in an earlier form, with a broader 2024 rollout across select stores, worth noting as a real, relatively recent addition rather than something that's always been part of the format.

## Other chains solved different problems

Kura Sushi isn't the only chain layering new technology onto Shiraishi's original format, and the other major names took the problem in different directions. Sushiro, one of the biggest competing chains, runs a second, separate express lane above or alongside the main belt specifically for tablet orders, a dedicated overhead track that delivers an ordered dish straight to a short roller conveyor at your table rather than making it travel the shared belt past every other diner first. Sushiro's plates carry their own IC chips too, used to automatically pull a plate after it's traveled 350 meters on the lane, a hard distance limit rather than Kura Sushi's timed-freshness approach. The billing step is its own piece of tech: rather than a human counting plates or scanning each one by hand, an AI camera mounted above the table identifies the color and number of plates a customer has taken and calculates the bill from that image alone.

## The format is quietly disappearing at other chains

Here's the part that surprised us researching this after the fact: the actual belt, the single defining feature of the whole format since 1958, is being phased out at some of the biggest competing chains, not because of nostalgia but because of real food waste. Chefs at a traditional kaiten-zushi restaurant prepare plates in batches and hope customers take them before the rice hardens and the fish loses its freshness, whatever doesn't get chosen in time gets pulled and thrown away. Kappa Sushi has reportedly switched all 299 of its locations to a made-to-order system with no plates pre-circulating on a belt at all, and Hamasushi runs a similar "straight lane" setup at 657 of its 681 stores, a real reported investment of roughly 7.2 billion yen specifically to cut an estimated 1,100 tons of food waste a year. Kura Sushi, notably, is the major holdout, still running a real physical belt chain-wide and managing the waste problem through the chip-tracking system described above instead of abandoning the belt entirely. Reporting on exactly what Sushiro has done is inconsistent between sources, some say it dropped belts chain-wide, others say it only stopped pre-placing unsold sushi on the belt since 2023, worth treating as unconfirmed rather than picking whichever claim sounds cleaner.

## What any of this means at your table

None of the history changes what a meal costs, we've already broken down Kura Sushi's real current plate pricing (110 to 150 yen per plate, most standard items around 115 yen) and the Bikkura Pon capsule-toy game on this site separately. What the history does change is how you read the table in front of you: the colored or patterned plate isn't decoration, it's a 67-year-old billing system doing its job in real time, and the dome covering each dish on newer-generation machines isn't a gimmick, it's a genuine food-safety mechanism with a chip inside actively timing how fresh what's underneath still is.

## Was it worth stopping to wonder about

Yes, in the way a lot of the best travel moments work: a kid's simple question turned a routine dinner into something we went and researched afterward, and the real answer (a 1958 Osaka restaurant, a legendary beer-conveyor inspiration, a patent that held an entire industry back for fifteen years) was more interesting than "it's just a conveyor belt" would have suggested. The format most visitors to Japan treat as a fun novelty has a real, specific, documented history behind it, worth the two minutes it takes to explain to a curious kid staring at plates going by.
