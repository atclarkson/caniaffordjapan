Before any sightseeing starts, arrival day has its own real cost, and it's one most trip budgets skip entirely. Here's a genuine, itemized Day 0 from a family of five landing in Tokyo, logged line by line rather than estimated after the fact.

## The real breakdown

- **Luggage forwarding (Yamato Transport):** 6,926 yen
- **Pocket wifi rental:** 8,008 yen
- **eSIM:** 1,939 yen
- **Accommodation:** 27,475 yen
- **Snacks and groceries:** 3,472 yen
- **Dinner:** 5,940 yen
- **Transportation:** 8,950 yen
- **Total:** 62,710 yen, roughly $400 at the exchange rate on the day this was logged

That's a real family-of-five total for the day you land, nothing more, nothing padded, and it's a useful anchor point even though individual categories (accommodation especially) swing a lot by where and when you're staying.

## The one line worth knowing about in advance

Luggage forwarding is the item most first-time visitors have never heard of, and it's worth planning around rather than discovering by accident. Yamato Transport and similar services (known generally as takuhaibin) will pick up your suitcases at the airport counter and deliver them directly to your hotel, usually arriving by early evening the same day if you check in before midday. The point isn't luxury, it's practical: instead of wrestling full-size suitcases through train transfers and station stairs with three kids in tow, you travel in with just a day bag and let your actual luggage catch up separately. Pricing varies by source and bag size, but a standard checked suitcase runs somewhere in the 2,000 to 3,200 yen range for an airport-to-hotel delivery, with service counters available at Narita, Haneda, Kansai International, and Chubu Centrair. Book at least a couple of days ahead if you're sending bags toward the airport instead of away from it, same-day airport drop-off for arriving passengers is the easier direction.

## The free step before any of the paid ones

Before any money changes hands, Visit Japan Web is worth setting up before you fly rather than at the airport. It's a free, browser-based system run by Japan's Digital Agency, no app download required, that lets you pre-register your immigration arrival card and customs declaration online ahead of time. The two have been combined into a single QR code, scannable at self-service kiosks available at Haneda, Narita, and Kansai, letting a family clear both immigration and customs at one machine instead of two separate lines. Registration needs your passport details, flight information, and your accommodation address in Japan, and it takes roughly 15 minutes, worth doing from home rather than fumbling through it on an airport wifi connection with tired kids. Complete it at least six hours before landing so the QR code is active when you arrive.

## Why day zero matters for budgeting

A trip budget that starts counting on day one of sightseeing misses a real cost that hits before any attraction, temple, or meal out has happened. Accommodation and transportation are the two line items doing most of the work here, together more than half the day's total, which tracks with how most multi-day trip budgets really shake out once arrival logistics are counted honestly instead of folded into "travel days don't really count." Building a full trip budget around attraction and food costs alone, without a real arrival-day number like this one, tends to undercount the actual total by a meaningful margin, especially for a family where every line item scales by headcount.

## Was the luggage forwarding worth it

For a family of five managing three kids through train transfers, yes, without much debate. The 6,926 yen charge on this particular day wasn't the cheapest version of the service, pricing depends on the distance, bag size, and number of pieces, but even the higher end of the per-bag range is a small price against the real difference between herding a loaded luggage cart down a station escalator with kids and walking onto a train carrying nothing but a backpack. It's the kind of cost that looks optional on a spreadsheet and stops looking optional the moment you're standing on a crowded platform with four suitcases and three children.
