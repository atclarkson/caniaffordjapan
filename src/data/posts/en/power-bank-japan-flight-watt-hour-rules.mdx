A phone that's dead by lunchtime from maps, photos, and a mobile Suica is a real problem on a Japan trip, and a power bank is the obvious fix. What's less obvious is that the thing in your bag is a lithium battery, and both the FAA and Japan's own airlines have specific rules about it, not a vague "ask at the gate" situation. Here's every rule that applies, line by line.

## The rules, line by line

- **Carry-on only, every time.** A power bank never goes in checked baggage, on any airline, full stop. Pack it in the bag that stays with you through security.
- **Under 100Wh: no real limit.** The FAA caps a single lithium-ion battery at 100 watt-hours for carry-on without needing anyone's permission, and most phone-sized power banks (10,000 to 20,000mAh) sit well under that line.
- **100 to 160Wh: capped at two, and only with the airline's OK.** ANA's current international rule allows up to two power banks in this range, or one under 100Wh plus one in the 100-160Wh band. JAL's page says the same ceiling applies, carry-on only.
- **Over 160Wh: not allowed on the plane at all.** Neither airline, nor the FAA, makes an exception here. This mostly catches large battery packs built for laptops or camping gear, not a phone-charging bank.
- **No charging it in flight.** JAL's rules specifically prohibit charging a power bank from the seat's own outlet or USB port mid-flight. Charge it on the ground, before you board.
- **The label has to show the rating, or it might not fly at all.** JAL's own guidance says that if the Wh rating can't be confirmed from what's printed on the bank, staff can refuse to let it on board. A power bank with a worn-off or missing label is a real risk, not a technicality.

## Doing the math on your own bank

The number that matters is watt-hours, not the mAh printed in big letters on the box. The formula, straight from JAL's own page: Wh equals the rated capacity in amp-hours times the rated voltage. Most power banks run at 3.7V internally, so a quick estimate is mAh divided by 1,000, times 3.7.

| Capacity | Rough Wh | Carry-on status |
|---|---|---|
| 10,000mAh | ~37Wh | Fine, no limit |
| 20,000mAh | ~74Wh | Fine, no limit |
| 27,000mAh | ~100Wh | Right at the line, check the printed rating |
| 40,000mAh+ | ~148Wh | Needs airline approval, capped at two |

Anything in the 10,000-20,000mAh range most people travel with clears the 100Wh line without the airline needing to say yes first. The rule only starts mattering once you're packing something built more for camping or laptop charging than topping up a phone.

## A setup that clears every rule without a form

A 20,000mAh bank per adult, charged the night before and packed into the same bag as passports and phones through security rather than the checked suitcase, covers a full day of maps, photos, and tap-to-pay transit with room to spare, and sits nowhere near either airline's approval threshold. There's no form to fill out or gate agent to convince at that size, which is the whole point of staying under the 100Wh line rather than buying the biggest bank on the shelf.

## The quick version

A normal phone-sized power bank, 10,000 to 20,000mAh, clears every limit without needing anyone's approval, just keep it in your carry-on and off the seatback charger mid-flight. Anything bigger, built for a laptop or camping gear rather than a phone, is the one case worth checking against ANA's or JAL's current baggage page before you pack it, since the rules cap out hard at 160Wh with no exceptions.
