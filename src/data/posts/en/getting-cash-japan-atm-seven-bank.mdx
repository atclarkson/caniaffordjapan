A Seven Bank ATM, the kind built into almost every 7-Eleven in the country, takes a foreign Visa, Mastercard, Maestro, Cirrus, UnionPay, American Express, JCB, Discover, or Diners Club card, is open 24 hours, and hands over up to ¥100,000 in a single withdrawal for a flat fee that's usually somewhere between 110 and 220 yen depending on the amount. That's the easy version. The fuller version has three network differences to know before you land, plus one place to avoid entirely.

## Seven Bank is the default, for a reason

Seven Bank's card page confirms the full network list directly: VISA, PLUS, Mastercard, Maestro, Cirrus, UnionPay, American Express, JCB, DISCOVER, and Diners Club are all accepted. That's a wider list than either of the other two options below, and with over 7-Eleven stores in nearly every neighborhood of every city, it's the ATM you'll walk past most often without looking for it.

The exact fee and per-transaction cap aren't published in plain numbers on Seven Bank's site, so take the figures here as well-sourced rather than confirmed straight from the bank: a ¥100,000 per-transaction limit comes up most often, with one source citing ¥50,000 instead, so treat your machine's on-screen limit as the answer on the day. The fee side runs ¥110 for smaller withdrawals up to around ¥220 or ¥275 for larger ones, on top of whatever your card issuer charges for a foreign ATM transaction. The screen shows the exact fee before you confirm, so you're never guessing at the register.

The daily limit, separate from the per-transaction cap, is set by your home bank, not by Seven Bank. If a withdrawal gets declined for no obvious reason, that's usually the first thing to check with your own bank before assuming the machine is broken.

One more card-specific note: pull cash with a debit card, not a credit card. Withdrawing on a credit card turns the transaction into a cash advance, which most issuers treat differently from an ordinary purchase, a separate and usually higher fee, plus interest that starts accruing from the moment of withdrawal rather than after a statement's grace period. Save the credit card for places that take cards directly and use a debit card for the ATM.

## When a 7-Eleven isn't the closest option

Two other networks cover most of the gaps:

Japan Post Bank ATMs sit inside post offices and are everywhere, over 20,000 of them nationwide, with English-language screens the same as Seven Bank's. The catch is hours: a post office branch ATM can close in the evening or over a weekend, which defeats the whole point if you need cash at 9pm. Lawson and FamilyMart's E-net machines fill some of that gap with English guidance, though not every machine in either chain's network accepts every card we've listed, a decline on one network's machine is worth a retry on a different one before assuming your card itself is the problem.

If you've already read our post on [Suica, Pasmo, and ICOCA](/blog/suica-pasmo-icoca-ic-card-japan/), the IC card covers transit and a lot of convenience-store purchases without cash at all. None of that replaces an ATM, you still need yen in hand for small shops, temples, and the gacha machines we cover in [our kids' debit card post](/blog/kids-debit-card-japan-cash-still-needed/), an ATM is just where that cash comes from.

## The one screen prompt that matters

Almost every one of these machines asks, at some point, whether to convert the withdrawal to your home currency right there on the spot or charge you in yen. Choose yen. Accepting the machine's conversion rate is a well-documented way to get a worse exchange rate than letting your own card issuer handle the conversion afterward. It's an easy prompt to tap through without reading, and it's the one tap on the whole transaction that costs you money if you get it wrong.

## Why the airport counter is the worse option

A currency exchange counter at Narita or Haneda is the most convenient place to get yen the moment you land, and that convenience is exactly what you're paying for. Multiple travelers comparing the two directly have found an ATM withdrawal landing noticeably better than a Narita counter's quoted rate on the same day, one specific comparison put a Seven Bank ATM at roughly 3% better than the airport counter after fees. The underlying interbank exchange rate doesn't change between the airport and a 7-Eleven three stops down the line, what changes is the markup a counter builds into its quoted rate versus the fee an ATM charges on top of a rate close to that same interbank number. A hotel front desk is worse again, often 5 to 10% off the rate you'd get from a card.

None of that means skip the airport entirely. Landing with zero yen and no working card is a problem. It means: change or withdraw just enough to get through the first few hours, a train ticket, a snack, cab fare if something goes wrong, then do your main cash withdrawals from a Seven Bank machine once you're past customs rather than loading up at the counter out of convenience.

## How much to pull out each time

Pulling the full ¥100,000 limit on day one and carrying it all trip-long is more cash than most families need to have on hand at once, and it's more to lose track of if a bag goes missing. A withdrawal every few days, enough to cover cash-only spots, gacha machines, and a buffer for anywhere a card gets declined, keeps the amount on hand reasonable without turning every evening into a hunt for an ATM. Each withdrawal still carries a flat fee regardless of size, so fewer, slightly larger withdrawals beat a lot of small ones if the fee itself is the part you're trying to minimize.

Run the math on a 10-day trip. One withdrawal near the ¥100,000 cap covers the whole stay for a single ¥110 to ¥220 fee, but means carrying six figures in yen through the back half of the trip. Three smaller withdrawals, say ¥30,000 to ¥40,000 each spaced a few days apart, costs three separate fees instead of one, maybe ¥330 to ¥660 total rather than ¥220, but keeps less cash in a bag at any one time. That's the actual tradeoff, a few hundred extra yen in fees against how much cash feels comfortable to carry, not a right or wrong answer.

## The quick version

A Seven Bank ATM inside a 7-Eleven, open 24 hours, takes the widest range of foreign cards of the three networks here, and charges a flat fee in the ¥110 to ¥220+ range per withdrawal, with a per-transaction cap most commonly cited around ¥100,000. Japan Post Bank and Lawson or FamilyMart's E-net machines work as backups when a 7-Eleven isn't close, with their hours and card-network quirks. Choose yen, not home-currency conversion, on the screen prompt. And skip the airport exchange counter beyond whatever gets you through the first hour, a 7-Eleven down the street from your hotel beats it on rate almost every time.
