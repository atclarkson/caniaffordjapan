Tokyo Skytree's official height, 634.0 meters exactly, wasn't picked because it beat a rival tower by a clean number or hit some round engineering target. It's wordplay. Musashi, the old province name for the region around Tokyo, breaks down as mu-sa-shi, and 6-3-4 reads the same way in Japanese, mu-sa-shi. The tower that became the tallest in the world was built to a height chosen for a pun on a province that stopped existing on any modern map more than a century ago.

## A four year build, finished on a leap day

Construction started in 2006 and ran three years and eight months, with the tower reaching its full 634 meter height in March 2011, the labor of roughly 585,000 people over the full project. It topped out as the world's tallest tower on completion, February 29, 2012, a leap day, and didn't open to the public until nearly three months later, May 22, 2012, once the broadcasting and observation facilities were finished out. It displaced Guangzhou's Canton Tower as the tallest tower on the planet and remains the second tallest structure in the world overall, behind only Dubai's Burj Khalifa.

## It replaced a tower that was still standing

The pun in the height almost distracts from the more practical reason Skytree exists at all. Tokyo Tower, the 332.9 meter broadcasting tower that still stands in Minato and that we've covered on this site separately, simply wasn't tall enough anymore. Tokyo's skyline kept growing through the 1990s and 2000s, and as the country planned its shift to digital terrestrial broadcasting, the newer high-rises around Tokyo Tower were blocking enough of its signal that large parts of the city couldn't get reliable reception. Skytree was built specifically to clear that problem and fully took over the city's main broadcasting duties from Tokyo Tower in 2013, standing nearly twice as tall as the tower it replaced, a genuine infrastructure fix that happened to also become the city's new tallest landmark and tourist draw.

## What it costs now, and why the number moves

Tokyo Skytree switched to dynamic pricing in April 2026, so the honest answer to "what does a ticket cost" is now a range rather than a flat number. The Tembo Deck alone, at 350 meters, runs from 1,800 yen for an adult booked in advance on a weekday, climbing to 2,000 yen on weekends and up to 2,300 yen for a same-day ticket on a busy date. The combo ticket adding the higher Tembo Galleria at 450 meters starts around 3,000 yen and moves between roughly 3,100 and 3,800 yen depending on the day and how far ahead you book. Kids 6 to 14 start at 900 yen for the deck alone, 1,500 yen for the combo. Buying in person at the fourth-floor counter instead of online adds a flat 500 yen service fee per ticket on top of whatever the day's price happens to be, worth booking ahead for that reason alone.

## Was it worth looking up at

We haven't been up the tower ourselves, our photos are both of it lit up at night from our own apartment window, a special illumination pattern rather than the standard nightly color rotation, though we can't say for certain which specific campaign or event it was marking. Even without a ticket, a tower engineered around a province-name pun and built by over half a million workers in under four years is worth knowing the real story behind, and the current dynamic pricing means the honest move now is checking the exact date on Skytree's own booking flow before assuming last year's number still holds.
