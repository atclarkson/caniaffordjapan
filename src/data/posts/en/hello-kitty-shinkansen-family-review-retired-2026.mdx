The platform at Shin-Osaka Station looks like any other until the train itself rounds the curve: not silver, not the usual blue-and-white, but a solid, unmistakable pink, Hello Kitty's bow painted the size of a window across the nose. Our three girls had already been told what was coming. Seeing it pull in was a different thing entirely, the kind of reaction that doesn't need describing beyond saying it was loud.

## The flight option nobody at the station was taking

Before any of the pink-train theming comes into it, Osaka to Fukuoka is also a route you can fly. Budget one-way fares between Itami and Fukuoka can start as low as around ¥7,450 on aggregator sites, roughly half the Shinkansen's reserved-seat fare, though that's a "from" headline price rather than a guaranteed JAL or ANA quote, worth checking the airlines' own sites rather than trusting the lowest number you see. The appeal stops there for a family, though: Itami sits well outside central Osaka, Fukuoka Airport is closer to downtown but still adds a transfer on both ends, and airport arrival windows, security, and baggage claim eat into whatever the ticket price saved. The Shinkansen runs city center to city center, no check-in window, no security line beyond walking through a gate, and lets three kids move around or nap in a way a middle airplane seat doesn't. We never seriously considered the flight.

## What's inside the pink cars

Only two of the train's eight cars carry the theming, and they're built for two different jobs. Car 1 was a display and gift-shop space, cases of Hello Kitty Shinkansen merchandise nobody could ride home without. Car 2 was the one worth boarding for, nicknamed the "Kawaii! Room," decorated floor to ceiling in Hello Kitty motifs with a dedicated photo spot featuring a Hello Kitty conductor figure. Cars 3 through 8 were standard Shinkansen seating, no theming at all, which matters if you've booked the wrong car and wonder why nothing looks pink. The ride itself from Shin-Osaka to Hakata runs about two and a half hours, stopping at Shin-Kobe, Himeji, Okayama, and Hiroshima along the way, the exact same route and the exact same 200-plus mph speeds as any other bullet train on the Sanyo Shinkansen line. The theming is cosmetic. The transportation underneath it is not.

## What it cost

Here's the part that surprises people who assume a character train comes with a character-train markup: it didn't. Reserved seats ran the same as any regular Shinkansen reservation on this route, somewhere in the ¥15,000 to ¥20,000 per adult range depending on exactly which fare class you book, a number that still lines up with current Shin-Osaka to Hakata reserved fares today (around ¥15,740 for a standard reserved seat as of this writing, ¥20,400 for Green Car). No upcharge for riding in the Hello Kitty cars specifically, no special ticket tier, just a normal seat reservation you pointed at cars 1 or 2 instead of letting the booking system assign you anywhere. The one real extra cost was optional: a limited-edition Hello Kitty ekiben, the boxed train lunch Japan does better than anywhere else, for about ¥1,200. Skip it and the whole themed experience costs exactly what the regular bullet train already costs.

## Why this review is also now a record of something that's gone

We filmed this ride in June 2023. Writing this up now, in October 2026, the most important fact about the Hello Kitty Shinkansen isn't the fare, it's that the train stopped running months ago. JR West retired it on Sunday, May 17, 2026, closing out just under eight years of service since it launched in June 2018, a partnership between JR West and Sanrio. The railway ran a real farewell campaign on the way out: Car 1, the old gift-shop car, was renamed "Thanks! Plaza" for the final stretch with new send-off artwork, and commemorative merchandise went on sale through the last day of service at the Kyoto Railway Museum, select JR West station shops, and online. If you're reading this hoping to book the exact ride we took, you can't, that specific pink train is retired equipment now, not a route you can search for on a booking site.

## What's still running if you want a Hello Kitty train today

The Shin-Osaka to Hakata Shinkansen wasn't the only themed train JR West ran. A separate set of Hello Kitty-decorated trains still operates on the Haruka line, the airport express connecting Kansai International Airport to Kyoto and Osaka, a shorter ride and a different kind of trip entirely rather than a cross-country Shinkansen journey. We've written up the [real Haruka cost breakdown separately](/blog/haruka-vs-rapit-kansai-airport-train-cost/) if that's the comparison you're trying to make for an airport transfer. Worth knowing before you plan around it, though: those Haruka trains are reportedly being phased out too, around summer 2027, so even this alternative has a real expiration date attached to it rather than being a permanent fixture.

## The booking mechanics that still apply to any Shinkansen ride

Even with this specific train gone, the practical side of what we learned carries over directly to booking any Shinkansen seat as a family. Reserved seating works the same whether or not a car happens to be decorated: you still book through the same systems, still pay the same base fare for the route and class, and the same luggage-forwarding services that move your bags ahead to your next hotel still apply regardless of which train you're on. We've written a [full comparison of where the real savings are on Shinkansen bookings](/blog/booking-shinkansen-tickets-japan-cheapest-way/), JR Pass versus Klook versus the station ticket machine, since that math hasn't changed and applies to every Shinkansen trip, themed or not.

## Was it worth it

At the time, yes, without hesitation. Paying zero premium for a themed car on a trip we were already taking, Osaka to Fukuoka, made it close to a free upgrade rather than a splurge decision. Our own honest read then still holds as a verdict now: if you're already riding this specific route, a themed car most travelers will never get to ride again is worth seeking out while it exists. The catch, obviously, is that this one no longer does. Treat this less as a recommendation and more as what a now-retired piece of Japanese train culture looked like, cost, and ran like from the inside, while three girls stared out a pink window at 200 miles an hour.
