Walking through Kyoto on our first night in the city, a six-and-a-half-meter mechanical crab waving its claws over a restaurant entrance is not a subtle sales pitch, and it worked exactly as designed. We hadn't planned on a crab dinner that evening. We also hadn't planned on the giant crab moving its eyestalks at us from above a doorway, and one of those two things changed our plans.

## The sign has its own history

That sign isn't unique to the Kyoto location. Kani Doraku opened its first restaurant in Dotonbori, Osaka, in 1960, the same spot that debuted the giant mechanized crab sign that's since been copied by half of Dotonbori's other restaurants. That original sign is about 6.5 meters tall, with arms and eyestalks that really move, and it's had a rough history of its own: a leg fell off and struck a customer in 1984, and in 2003 fans of the Hanshin Tigers baseball team climbed it during a championship celebration and made off with its eyes. The chain expanded across Japan from that one Dotonbori location, Kyoto's branch among them, each one keeping the same oversized crab out front as a built-in advertisement nobody can walk past without noticing.

Kyoto's location sits at the intersection of Teramachi-dori and Sanjo-dori, a covered shopping street corner rather than the open neon strip the Osaka original occupies along the Dotonbori canal. Same chain, same crab-shaped promise above the door, a noticeably different setting once you're standing in front of it.

## What the menu charges

We don't have a receipt from that night, no itemized bill to quote here, just the real reaction caught on camera and a dinner we clearly weren't expecting to spend that kind of evening on. What we can quote honestly is the Kyoto location's current published menu: five crab kaiseki courses, all nine dishes each, tax included. Hinata runs 4,730 yen, Misaki 5,280 yen, Koharu 5,720 yen, Nagisa 6,050 yen, and Yuzuki tops the list at 7,260 yen. Every tier gets you the same nine-course structure, the difference between tiers is the quality and cut of crab used rather than the number of dishes on the table.

## What nine dishes of crab looks like

Every tier follows the same real structure: crab sashimi to start (raw king or snow crab), a vinegared crab dish, grilled king crab brought to the table lightly charred rather than heavily seasoned, kani-suki, the chain's own crab hot pot simmered with tofu and vegetables in a light broth, crab tempura, and crab-based rice or sushi to close it out, dessert included. The higher tiers upgrade the cut and grade of crab used across those same nine courses rather than adding extra dishes, so Yuzuki at 7,260 yen isn't a longer meal than Hinata at 4,730 yen, it's the same nine-course structure built from better crab.

## What that means for a family of five

Run the real math against our own group size rather than guessing: two adults and three kids at even the entry-level Hinata course would land around 23,650 yen before drinks, assuming every kid orders a full adult course, which isn't a given at a sit-down kaiseki restaurant, some crab restaurants offer a scaled-down kids' plate at a lower price, worth asking about at the door rather than assuming the adult tiers are the only option. Step up to the mid-range Koharu course for the table and that number moves to 28,600 yen. The top Yuzuki tier for five would run 36,300 yen, a genuine splurge-tier Kyoto dinner, in the same territory as the most expensive meal we've written about from a teppanyaki counter in Sakai.

## Book ahead, don't walk in on hope

Kani Doraku is well known enough across Japan that walking in without a reservation, especially for a group of five, is a real gamble on dinner timing rather than a safe bet. We weren't planning that dinner ahead of time, the sign made the decision for us, and a party our size without a reservation means accepting whatever wait the host gives you rather than picking your own evening. If a crab dinner is already on your Kyoto itinerary rather than a spur-of-the-moment stop, booking ahead through the restaurant's own site or a hotel concierge avoids that risk entirely.

## Was it worth it

We'd do it again, and specifically because we didn't plan it. A sign that works that hard to get you through the door is doing exactly what Kani Doraku built it to do back in 1960, and a crab kaiseki dinner in Kyoto is a real, worthwhile splurge if you're going to have one on a trip anyway. Walk in with the real course prices above in mind rather than ordering blind off a menu that doesn't translate its own price tiers clearly, and it's a memorable night on your own terms rather than a bill that surprises you at the end of it.
