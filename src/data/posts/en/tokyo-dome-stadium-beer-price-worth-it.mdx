A different Yomiuri Giants game than the one we've already written up, this one a May 2024 evening at Tokyo Dome, and the photo that stuck with us wasn't a play on the field. It was the beer in hand a few innings in, poured tableside by a vendor working the aisle with a keg strapped to their back, the way Japanese stadiums have always done it and American ones never have.

Here's the part that matters for anyone budgeting a night at the Dome: that price has moved since our photo was taken. Tokyo Dome's own pricing notice confirms a vendor-poured draft went from 800 to 1,000 yen starting in March 2026. The vendors themselves, mostly students and part-timers working the aisles, earn a base wage plus a small commission per cup sold, historically around 34 yen at the old price, incentive enough that some manage to move 300 cups in a single game.

What you get for that yen isn't just the beer. It's a souvenir cup, yours to keep, and a different in-stadium experience than buying from a concession counter and walking back to your seat. No line, no missed innings, the beer comes to you between pitches, sometimes mid-inning if the vendor's working your section.

It's also not the only brand on offer. Different vendors carry different labels, Asahi and Sapporo both make regular rounds through the stands alongside Suntory's, and prices across the twelve professional teams' home stadiums have moved in step with each other over the past couple of seasons rather than Tokyo Dome standing out as the expensive outlier. If beer isn't the draw, concession stands elsewhere in the Dome sell the usual ballpark food at prices that still read as cheap next to a typical American stadium, which is the more common reaction from first time visitors than any complaint about the beer specifically.

## So, worth it?

At 1,000 yen, a Tokyo Dome beer costs roughly double what the same brand runs at a convenience store a few blocks away. Judged purely on the liquid, that's not a great trade, and nobody's pretending stadium markup is a bargain anywhere in the world. Judged on the whole moment, a vendor pouring it right at your seat, a souvenir cup, zero minutes lost from the game, it's the kind of markup we don't mind paying for once we're already through the gate and settled in for nine innings. We'd pay it again on our next trip back. We just wouldn't act surprised anymore when the number's gone up from what we remembered paying last time, which at a stadium is less a Tokyo Dome quirk than just how ticketed venues work everywhere.
