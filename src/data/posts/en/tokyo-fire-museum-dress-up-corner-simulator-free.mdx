We already wrote up the Tokyo Fire Museum's basement full of vintage trucks and its climb-in rooftop helicopter. What that review left out entirely: a third-floor wing built around a kids' firefighter dress-up corner and a fire-extinguishing simulator, two more free activities in the same building we never got around to showing.

## The dress-up corner itself

On the museum's third floor, kids can change into miniature firefighter uniforms, helmets included, free to try on and free to photograph in, no separate ticket or sign-up sheet required beyond showing up. It sits alongside a fire truck exhibit kids can explore the inside of, one fitted with a working siren rather than a silent static display, a detail that turns a quiet exhibit room into something considerably louder and more memorable for a group of kids under 10.

## The simulator we didn't mention either

Past the dress-up corner, the same floor runs a fire-extinguishing simulation activity, visitors get a chance to practice putting out a simulated fire after watching a diorama presentation, plus a driving simulator themed around operating a fire engine. Neither requires anything beyond what's already included in the museum's free admission, the same zero-yen entry that covers the basement trucks and rooftop helicopter our original review focused on.

## Part of a bigger, real pattern across Tokyo

This simulator wing isn't a one-off idea unique to the Yotsuya museum. Tokyo runs a real network of free disaster-prevention learning centers built around the same hands-on approach, the Tokyo Fire Department's own Ikebukuro Life Safety Learning Center, housed inside a working fire station, lets visitors spray a screen showing a simulated kitchen fire, and the Honjo center combines an earthquake simulator with the same kind of extinguisher practice. Japan's frequent earthquakes and historical fire risk are the real reason this kind of training exists at all, and it starts young, disaster education shows up in Japanese schools from early grades on, which is part of why a museum aimed partly at kids treats hands-on simulation as a core exhibit rather than an afterthought tacked onto the vehicle displays.

## Why this wing matters as much as the one we already covered

Our first visit leaned hard into the vehicles, real fire trucks spanning a century, a helicopter kids could sit in, because that's what grabbed attention walking in the door. The dress-up and simulator wing rewards a slightly longer visit, the kind where a family has already seen the trucks and isn't ready to leave yet. For three kids with different attention spans, the costume corner in particular turned into its own extended stop, less about learning firefighting history and more about imagination play with real props rather than toys, free to repeat as many times as patience allowed.

## Still the same free museum

Nothing about the cost changes here, the museum's admission covers everything in the building, trucks, helicopter, dress-up corner, and simulator alike, no upsell, no premium wing hiding behind a second ticket. We've already covered the practical details, hours, accessibility, how to get there, in the original review rather than repeating them here; this is purely the part of the same free visit that review didn't get to.

## Was the extra wing worth finding

Completely, and it's the kind of thing that only shows up by walking the whole building rather than assuming a small museum has nothing left after the headline exhibit. A free museum that turns out to have two separate kid-focused wings instead of one is worth more than its zero-yen price tag already suggested, and worth going back to specifically for the half most visitors, including our own first write-up, walk past on the way to the trucks.
