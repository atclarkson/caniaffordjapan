Every flight-booking guide promises a trick. Most of what moves the price is free to do and takes ten minutes; most of what inflates the final number is an airline fee you agree to without reading closely at checkout. Splitting those two apart is more useful than another "best time to book" headline.

## What's free

**Flexible-date search.** Nearly every comparison tool, including Skyscanner, has a whole-month or date-range view instead of a fixed two dates. Shifting departure by a few days, or searching a two to three week spread, is consistently cited as one of the biggest real levers on price, and it costs nothing but a few extra clicks.

**Checking alternate airports.** Searching Haneda alongside Narita, or your nearest second-choice home airport alongside your main one, costs nothing and regularly turns up a cheaper combination than the obvious first search.

**Fare alerts.** Setting a price-tracking alert on a route and waiting is free. It won't always find a deal, but it costs nothing to leave running in the background while you watch a price before committing.

**Knowing the real cheap months.** Multiple sources agree on the general shape even if they differ on exact weeks: late January through early March, and September, run cheapest. Cherry blossom season (late March into early April), Golden Week (late April into early May), and the December holiday stretch carry real premiums, with one source estimating 45 to 90% above the February baseline. None of that costs anything to know, it's just worth checking before you lock in dates around a specific event.

**Booking in the right window.** Estimates vary by source, but the general range is 2 to 4 months out for an ordinary week and 5 to 6 months out for a peak one. Booking 8-plus months ahead generally isn't worth the wait, prices that far out tend to be speculative guesses rather than real low fares.

**The long-layover trick.** Some airlines price a routing with a long stopover, 12 to 48 hours in a hub like Seoul or Taipei, at or below what a shorter connection on the same route costs, because it's filling an off-peak leg rather than a premium nonstop slot. Korean Air and EVA Air both get cited for this on their own multi-city search tools. This one comes with a real caveat: the sourcing on it is thinner than the rest of this list, mostly travel blogs rather than airline-published policy, and nothing in what we found confirms current terms on a specific Japan routing. Worth a search on the airline's own site before you plan around it, not something to book blind.

## What isn't free, even when it looks like it should be

**The incognito mode myth.** This one's free to try and doesn't cost anything, but it also doesn't work, worth clearing up since it still gets repeated constantly. A 2016 Consumer Reports test of 372 flight searches found prices matched in regular versus incognito browsing about 88% of the time, with incognito coming out more expensive slightly more often than it came out cheaper. Separately, economists at Berkeley, Yale, and Chicago studied one major airline's pricing data directly and found an individual shopper's search history had no measurable effect on the fare they were shown. Prices move because of dynamic, demand-driven pricing across the whole market, not because your browser is being tracked personally. The myth persists because someone checks a fare, checks again later in a private window, sees a different number, and credits the wrong cause, ordinary price movement over time looks like incognito working if you don't control for the gap between searches.

**Checked bags.** This is where airlines make back what flexible-date searching saves you. ANA's international routes run on a piece-based allowance, with extra pieces beyond what's printed on your ticket running 5,000 to 20,000 yen each. JAL reportedly allows two free 23 kg bags in Economy and Premium Economy on international routes, with an excess-piece fee around 20,000 yen (roughly $200) between Japan and the Americas or Europe, confirm that figure on JAL's own site before counting on it. US carriers moved in the same direction in 2026: major US airlines raised checked bag fees together, with a first bag running around $45 each way and a second around $55, on top of the ticket price, not included in it.

**Seat selection.** On a lot of fares, especially the lowest "basic economy" tier US and Japanese carriers both sell, picking your own seat costs extra, and the airline will auto-assign you something during check-in if you don't pay for it. For a family trying to sit together, that fee is effectively not optional, it just moves from "price per seat" to "price to not get split up across the cabin."

**Basic economy's other restrictions.** The cheapest fare class on most routes usually comes with no changes allowed, no refunds, boarding last, and sometimes no overhead bin space included either. The headline price looks like the deal; the real price, once you add a bag and a seat and the flexibility a family needs, often lands close to the standard economy fare you skipped past to find it.

**Trip insurance upsells at checkout.** We've written before about why we don't make insurance recommendations on this site, the same caution applies here: a booking site's default-checked insurance add-on at checkout is a real cost, not a free safety net, decide on it deliberately rather than letting a pre-ticked box decide for you.

## What a real ticket costs right now

Rough 2026 estimates put a round-trip economy fare from the US West Coast to Japan around $900 to $1,300, and from the East Coast around $1,100 to $1,600, both rising well past that during peak weeks. Premium economy adds roughly $800 to $1,400 on top of the economy fare, and business class adds $3,000 to $5,000. These are estimates from travel-booking sources, not a guaranteed quote, treat them as a planning range and check live fares closer to your actual dates.

## What the add-ons do to a family's total

A single traveler barely notices a $45 bag fee next to a $1,000 ticket. A family of five notices immediately, the add-ons scale with headcount the same way the base fare does:

| Item | Per person | Family of five |
|---|---|---|
| Base fare (West Coast, off-peak estimate) | ~$900 | ~$4,500 |
| First checked bag each way | ~$45-55 | ~$225-275 |
| Seat selection (if not included) | ~$30-80 | ~$150-400 |
| Total before trip insurance | varies | roughly $4,875-5,175 |

The base fare row is the one headline price gets built around; the other two rows are the ones that turn a "cheap flight" search result into the real number on the card statement. None of these figures are fixed, carriers and fare classes vary the specifics, but the shape holds: a bag-and-seat gap that's easy to shrug off solo is hundreds of real dollars once five people are flying.

## For a family of five

None of the free strategies above change much on their own, but stacked together they add up: searching flexible dates across September instead of locking into a single fixed week in April, checking Haneda as well as Narita, and setting a fare alert rather than booking the first number you see, cost nothing and can meaningfully move a family's total airfare. The paid side is where five people instead of one makes the real difference, a $45 bag fee or a seat-selection charge that's trivial for a solo traveler multiplies by five fast, which is exactly why it's worth checking what's included in a fare before comparing it to a cheaper-looking one that charges for everything separately.
